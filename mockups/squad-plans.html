More than one squad, and a way to tell them apart

A plan library: build a fifteen, name it, keep it, and start another. Then tick two or three and get an answer about which one you should actually field — not a side-by-side table you have to read yourself.

Every number below is real. Three squads built by the site's own optimiser under £100m and the three-per-club rule, projected over GW1–6 by xpForGw, and compared over 20,000 paired simulated runs · nothing here is illustrative

Duplicate is the one that matters. Nobody builds a second squad from an empty pitch — they build the first one, wonder about one decision, and want to change it without losing the original. Copy-then-edit is the actual workflow, so it is one tap from the card rather than buried in a menu.
Where it lives, and whether you need an account. You do not. Each plan is a row in fpl_plans in your browser — the same place the single squad lives now — so it costs nothing, needs no login, and works offline. The price is that it is per-device: plans made on a laptop do not appear on a phone. A share code covers that without an account, the same way the squad import already works: it packs the fifteen and the week decisions into a short string you paste on the other device. If plans ever need to follow you around automatically, that is when an account earns its keep, and not before.

How often does the better plan actually win?

Each pair simulated 20,000 times with common random numbers — every player draws the same score in both plans, so the eight players the squads share cancel out exactly and what is left is the decision you are actually making. This is the panel the feature exists for, and it is the one nobody else draws. "Plan A projects 11.7 more" sounds decisive. "Plan A wins 63% of the time" is the same fact and does not, and the second one is true. Better than a coin toss, a long way from a certainty, and the Haaland build still comes out ahead in more than a third of runs — usually the ones where he does what he is bought to do.
Why common random numbers and not two independent runs. These squads share eight players. Simulated separately, those eight draw different scores in each plan and add about eighty points of noise that has nothing to do with the decision — the 12-point edge would vanish inside it and every comparison would come out 50/50. Drawing them once and reusing the number in both plans removes exactly the part the two squads have in common. It is the difference between a panel that answers the question and a panel that looks like it does.

Buying Haaland is buying four captaincies, not one striker. He takes the armband in four of six weeks, which is the real reason the plan feels different despite projecting eleven points worse — and it is also why its distribution is the widest of the three. A captain is a doubled bet, so the plan with the most concentrated armband has the most to gain and the most to lose, and the table on its own never says that.
Note what the third plan's GW5 captain is: Thiaw, a £5.0m defender. That is not the model being clever, it is the model telling you the plan has no captain — the best available doubled score is a defender, and a week with no armband worth having is a real finding about a squad, not a rendering glitch.
How it would work

Four things to build, in order

1 · storage

fpl_plans becomes a list of {id, name, base, weeks, startGw, updated}. The existing single squad migrates in as "Plan 1" on first load. Nothing else changes — the planner already stores exactly this shape, just once.

2 · the library

A strip above the pitch: active plan, switcher, duplicate, rename, delete. Duplicate is the primary action. Everything below it keeps working on whichever plan is active.

3 · compare

Tick two to four, and the Insights tab gains a Compare group. Every panel already there takes a squad; most of them take two with a colour each and no new maths.

4 · head to head

The one genuinely new piece: the paired simulation. It reuses the draw function the floor and ceiling panel already has, run over two lineups with a shared random stream.

What this is not, and will not pretend to be. These are six-week projections of a fixed fifteen. They do not know about the transfers you will make in week three, injuries that have not happened, or price changes. Beyond GW1 no fixture is priced by the market, so weeks 2–6 lean on last season's team strengths adjusted for the odds banked so far. A plan comparison is a statement about the squads as they stand today, and it should be re-read after every deadline rather than treated as a season-long verdict — which is exactly why the plans are stored and editable rather than scored once and filed.
