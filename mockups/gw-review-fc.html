Matchday

Fifteen directions so far and every one was a document — a newspaper, a bulletin, a lab report, a sticker page. This set stops being printed and speaks the language the audience is already looking at on the same weekend. Ultimate Team's card rarity is ownership by another name, which is the whole reason it fits: under one per cent owned is a differential, over twenty-five is the template everybody already had.

A

The eleven

Who actually returned, and how many people had them. The best legal side of the gameweek, on the pitch, as cards. The rating is the points. The rarity is the ownership — and in gameweek one that produces a squad of four differentials, five rares and one player the whole game already owned, which is the review's finding drawn rather than written.

What it is

Six numbers on every card, all of them real: minutes, goals, assists, bonus, BPS and defensive contributions. No invented pace rating, and no badges, kits or portraits — where a crest would go there is a three-letter code, which the site owns and which reads better at 40px anyway.

The risk. It flatters the week it draws. A gameweek where the template all hauled produces eleven bronze cards and looks like a bug. That is survivable — bronze everywhere is itself the story that week — but the page has to be built to say so.

Before this ships. The card silhouette is a recognisable piece of another product's design language. Nothing here copies an asset — no crest, no kit, no portrait, no typeface of theirs — but the shipped page should not name the game either, the way this mockup does in order to explain itself. Same shape, our own words.

B

The model, played against the gameweek. The post-match stats board, where the two sides are the eleven the ratings would have picked and the eleven that actually existed. It is the most honest fixture the site can put on, because it is the only one where the model can lose.

What it is

The scoreline is the argument: the model's favourites cost £29.5m more and scored 81 fewer, and the side that actually won the week was projected 34.0 against their 58.4. Bars run outward from the centre so the losing side of each row is visible as length, not as a smaller number you have to read.

The risk. A perfect XI is hindsight and nobody could have owned it, so a scoreline framing it as a defeat is unfair to a model that only ever claimed an average. The sub-line under each side does the honest work — cost and ownership — and stops 67–148 reading as failure.

What it is

The only one of these four that could carry the whole page on its own, and the only one that degrades gracefully — in a dull gameweek it is still a hub, just with duller numbers in it. Every tile is a real figure with a real sentence under it, which is what stops a dashboard becoming decoration.

The risk. Tiles are the safest thing here and the least memorable. It wins on a phone and loses on anyone's screenshot.

D

The walkout

One card, revealed. The gameweek's real story is not the top scorer, it is the player the model ranked last of all 495 and who then scored fifteen. That deserves the treatment the format reserves for the pull nobody expected, rather than a row in a table.

What it is

A moment rather than a layout, and the argument for it is that the model's worst call is more interesting than its best and almost nothing on the web publishes it. Doing that once a week, at the top of the review, buys more trust than any accuracy figure.

The risk. It is one screen of animation carrying one fact, so it cannot be the review — it is the opening of it, with C underneath. Motion is off entirely under prefers-reduced-motion, and the card and every number are in the markup, so with no animation at all the panel still reads.
