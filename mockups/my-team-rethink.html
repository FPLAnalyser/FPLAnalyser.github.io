Six ways to build My Team

The last set was one idea — tabs — refined four times. These are six different answers to the same question, three of which throw navigation out entirely. Same squad, same numbers, read out of site_data/2026-27/ tonight. GW1's opening fixture is now played, so the results in here are real: Arsenal 3 Coventry 0, pulled at 23:41. Anything not yet knowable is stamped.

01

The feed

No tabs, no sections, no navigation at all. One column of cards, ordered by how much they matter right now, recomputed every time the page loads. A card about your captain being injured is at the top on Friday and gone by Sunday; your season rank sits at the bottom for eleven months. The page has no fixed shape — the week gives it one. For: nothing is ever more than one screen away, and it is the only structure that works identically on a phone and a desktop. Against: if the ordering is wrong even twice, people stop trusting the top of the list, and there is nothing else to fall back on. It also has nowhere to put something you want to browse rather than be told.

The number in the tile is the urgency score. It is the thing that would need building — everything else here already exists.

02

The pitch is the page

Everything lives on the player it is about. There are no panels, tables or charts — tap a shirt and it widens in place to show the projection broken into its parts, the fixture, and the one swap worth making. Close it and the pitch is whole again. For: it matches how people actually think about their team, which is player by player, and the site already has every number it needs per player. Against: anything about the squad as a whole — concentration, budget, the shape of the next five weeks — has no home at all. It is a beautiful answer to half the problem.

Try it: the shirts are buttons. O'Shea is the interesting one.
03

Questions, not data

Navigate by what you came to find out. Not "Live", "Planner", "Review" — those are names for where data is kept. These are the eight things a manager actually opens the app to settle, each with its answer already computed and visible in the header, so the page is readable without opening anything. For: every row is a claim the site is willing to make, which is the whole product in one screen. Against: the questions are fixed and yours may not be on the list — and a question the model cannot answer well still has to sit there with something next to it.
04

The scrubber

One control, and it is time. Drag left into gameweeks that have happened and the page is a review; drag right into ones that have not and it is a planner. There is no boundary between the two because there is no boundary in the season — the same eleven players, the same projections, just aimed at a different week. The handle starts on the gameweek that is live. For: one interaction replaces a navigation hierarchy, and "what does GW5 look like" stops being three clicks. Against: a slider is a poor way to reach a specific place, it is fiddly with a thumb, and it hides everything you are not currently pointing at.

Try it: drag the handle.

05

Keep / change / watch

Shaped like the decision, not like the data. Every one of your fifteen sits in exactly one column, placed there by the model, and the entire job of the page is moving players between them. Nothing is displayed that does not help you make or resist that move. For: it is the only one of these six where the page has a completed state — three columns you agree with — so you can finish and leave. Against: it is opinionated to the point of being bossy, and it is wrong the moment the projection is. Being told to sell someone who then scores is a worse experience than working it out yourself.

Placement rule as drawn: below 2.5 projected is Change, below 4.0 with a hard fixture is Watch, everything else is Keep. Real numbers, deliberately crude threshold — the point is the shape.

06

You against the template

Your team only matters relative to everyone else's. Points do not win the game, rank does — so the page opens as a diff against the most-owned eleven and expresses everything as where you differ and what that difference is worth. Eight players in, eight players out. For: it answers the question people actually care about, which is not "how many points" but "am I gaining or losing ground". Against: it quietly encourages you to converge on the template, which is how you finish exactly average — and it is dispiriting in a week like this one, where the template is ahead.
