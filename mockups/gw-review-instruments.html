Four instruments of record

The eleven directions before these were all, underneath, someone presenting the gameweek. These four stop presenting. Each is a document made by somebody whose job is to check whether a prediction held — a forecast office, a pathology lab, an operator with a red pen, an Isotype draughtsman counting people. Every number is computed from the 495 GW1 projections joined to actuals on player code.

A

The verification

Was the forecast any good? A weather office never claims its forecast was good — it publishes the forecast, publishes the outcome and marks the gap in a house style so plain nobody can accuse it of dressing up a bust. A site that publishes p60 owes the reader the same page.

Summary

The forecast held at the bottom of the scale and drifted warm at the top. Of the 95 players given a 90% + chance of an hour, 82% played one; of the 40 given 80–90%, 78% did. The worst band is 70–80%, where 58% started — seventeen points below the middle of its own band, and the only band flagged bust. Below 50% the forecast is well behaved, and the 185 players written off at under 10% are close to exact: 10% of them played the hour.

What it is

The reliability diagram, in the only register that suits it. Bars over 45° lines are how forecasters have marked their own homework since the 1950s; the design work here is the restraint — one grey for both bars, and the only colour on the page is the shortfall, because the shortfall is the story.

The risk. Ten bands over one gameweek is thin. Six of them have fewer than forty players in them, and a single postponed fixture would move the 30–40% band by ten points. The footer says so rather than hiding it, and by GW6 the same layout is reading a real sample.

B

The reference range

Which calls were abnormal? A blood panel puts a measurement, a normal band and a flag on one line, and a clinician reads forty of them in seconds. It also happens to be the only layout that reveals the model's real shape: the error band is asymmetric, because points have a floor at zero and no ceiling at all.

Interval derivation. Empirical, not theoretical: the central 80% of this gameweek's own 495 errors, so 397 results fall inside it by construction. It is quoted to show the asymmetry — the band runs 2.55 points below the projection and 3.71 above, and the extremes are wider still. The worst overshoot was +14.99; the worst undershoot, −4.59. A projection cannot be wrong by more than it forecast on the downside, and can be wrong without limit on the up.

Not a diagnosis. A flag marks a result outside the interval, not a fault. Mendy was ranked 495th of 495 and scored 15; nothing in the underlying data made that findable.

What it is

Every row on one shared scale from −5 to +15, so the page reads as a single measurement rather than fourteen little bars. The pale band is the reference interval; it sits off to the left, which is the finding — the model's misses are not symmetrical and no symmetric error bar would have said so.

The risk. Clinical framing can read as cold, or worse, as a claim to medical authority the numbers do not have. The footer does the work: it says the interval is drawn from this gameweek's own errors and that a flag is not a fault.

What it is

The only one of the eleven directions in which the model gets marked rather than displayed. That is the argument for it: a site that publishes numbers every week needs a page where the numbers are visibly answerable to something, and a red pen says that faster than any amount of copy about accuracy.

The risk. Nostalgia is a one-week joke unless the format carries information, so the pen only ever writes what happened and the strike-through only ever means nought minutes. The pen lives in its own grid column, not floated over the type — an earlier attempt absolutely-positioned the annotations and Dewsbury-Hall's line ran straight underneath one.

D

Isotype

How many, of what? Otto Neurath's rule, and the reason this is not simply a chart with icons on it: a greater quantity is shown by more signs, never a bigger sign. Which is the honest way to draw a gameweek, because the unit being counted is a person.

Who owned the best eleven
The highest-scoring legal side of the week cost £63.0m and made 148 points. Its average owner was eight people in a hundred.
One square = one manager in a hundred  ·  mean ownership 8.1%
Why this and not a bar chart. A bar says 495 and 196 are lengths. A row of figures says they are people, which is what makes the second row land — you can see the grey block, and the grey block is the reason the model's average error among players who did not appear is 0.69 and among players who played an hour is 2.69. Counting in people also forbids the trick this page most wanted to pull: there is no way to make Mendy's fifteen points look bigger than anybody else's fifteen.

What it is

1930s Vienna, drawn by Gerd Arntz in Futura — Jost here, being the nearest face on a font host. Flat colour, no gradient, no perspective, no scaled pictogram anywhere. It is the most legible of these four on a phone, which is where most of this will be read on a Sunday night.

The risk. Isotype only works on counts. It cannot draw xP, or a captain call, or a fixture, so it can never be the whole review — it is the opening spread, and A or B does the accounting underneath it.
