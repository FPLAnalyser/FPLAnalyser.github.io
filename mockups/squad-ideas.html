Idea 1 — the honest scoreboard

Projected against actual, cumulative

Fifteen real players over the last eight gameweeks of 2025/26: what the site's own expected-points figure said they should score, against what they actually did. The shaded area is the running gap.

ProjectedActualRunning over/under
Unlucky or wrong — the only chart that answers it. These fifteen finished on 395 real points against a 372 projection, +6.1% over eight weeks. A gap that oscillates around zero is variance; a gap that trends is a model that has stopped describing your squad, and it is the difference between holding and selling. The catch: the projection line here is each player's season-average expected points, because a walk-forward projection for weeks already played cannot be reconstructed after the fact. Doing this properly means logging each gameweek's projection before the deadline — which is why it is the one item on this page with a date attached.

Chips are an optimal-stopping problem, not a hunch. The values here are close together — Bench Boost swings only 12.8 to 14.7 across six weeks — which is itself the finding: in a normal run of fixtures no week is special, and the right play is to hold for a double gameweek. The chart earns its keep by making a flat profile visible, so you stop looking for a peak that is not there.

The general form of the defensive-concentration chart. That one covered clean sheets; this covers everything, attack included. FPL returns are correlated inside a club far more than managers price — a side that wins 4–0 pays its defenders, its keeper, its midfielders' clean-sheet point and its forwards at once. Knowing that 16% of your projection depends on one club's results is the kind of thing you want to have decided deliberately.
