Mockups · GW Review, football's own visual language

Four more, and none of them from a design system

The last set reached for charts and newspapers. This one reaches for the places football results have actually been printed and displayed — the board at the ground, the telly on a Saturday, the album in the playground, and the weekend itself as a shape in time. Same measured gameweek behind every one.

A

The board

A split-flap departure board, which is what a results board is. Amber on black, condensed caps, every character in its own cell with the hinge line through it. It is the single most legible way anyone has found to show a column of scores at a distance, which is exactly the job — and it makes an ordinary results list feel like standing in a ground. For: unmistakable at a glance, works at any size, and carries the one thing a review must not bury, which is the scores. Against: a board shows results and nothing else. Every analytical thing this site does well has to live somewhere underneath it, so it is a masthead rather than a page.

B

Teletext

Page 302, where a generation actually read the football results. Forty columns, seven colours, double-height headers, and not one pixel spent on decoration — a constraint so tight it forces the writing to be good. Everything on this page is a real GW1 number set in a grid that cannot hold a chart, a photograph or an excuse. For: nobody else in fantasy football looks remotely like this, it is free to render, and the format's own discipline is the best editor a weekly page could have. Against: it is a joke to anyone under thirty five, the colour palette fails contrast in at least two of its seven, and forty columns is a hard ceiling on how much can ever be said.
C

The album

The team of the week as a sticker page. Football's own native visual language, and the one metaphor that already encodes ownership: the eleven that returned are stickers, the ones most of the game owned are marked got, and the best player in the gameweek is the shiny — the foil one everybody wanted and almost nobody had. De Cuyper was owned by 1.7%, which is exactly what a rare sticker is. For: the metaphor does the analysis for you, it is the most shareable object on any of these lists, and it is warm in a way a dashboard never is. Against: it flatters rarity, which is not the same as being right, and a page that treats a bad gameweek as a fun sticker page will read as tone-deaf about eleven times a season.

Six of the eleven were owned by under two per cent of the game. The foil is the highest score in the gameweek; the faded ones are the players most managers already had, which in gameweek one was exactly two of the eleven.

D

The weekend

A gameweek is four days long and nobody ever draws it that way. Friday teatime to Monday night on one axis, every fixture pinned where it actually kicked off, the stem's height being the points the best player in it took. It reads as a shape: a single fixture on Friday, the Saturday wall, a Sunday pair and then one game alone on Monday holding everybody's week open. For: it is the only view that answers "when did my gameweek happen", it makes a Monday-night differential visible as a structural fact, and it is the natural home for a live version — the line fills up as the weekend runs. Against: without goal times it is fixtures on a timeline rather than events on one, and the honest version of that needs a data source the site does not have.

The stems are the best individual return in each fixture. Saturday afternoon carried five games and three of the week's four biggest hauls; Monday carried one fixture and Chelsea's Palmer, which is why the review could not be written until Tuesday morning.

Measured. Scores, expected goals and best players rebuilt from actuals/gw1.json; kick-off times from availability.json; ownership and projections from the file frozen before the deadline. Joined on code, never on name.
