The season is the spine

Scrap the last set. Picking a fifteen is a fortnight's job and I built a whole mode around it; for the other thirty-six weeks everybody already has a team and is changing it. So here is the page built from the three things that actually matter — the squad, the planning, and the timeline of weeks with what you scored and what you are projected to score — with the timeline promoted from a strip above the board to the thing the page is organised around.

The idea underneath all four

One line, GW1 to GW38, past and future on the same axis

The strip today only looks forward, and only twelve weeks. But the most useful thing a season view can do is put what you scored and what you are projected to score on one continuous line, with now marked — so the plan is not a separate object from the record, it is the same line continuing. Transfers, hits and chips are events drawn on it, which means your whole season plan is legible at a glance instead of being something you have to step through to remember.

Everything here exists already — the projection, the GW rating, the hard count, the chip plan, the transfers per week. None of it has ever been drawn on one axis. Two things this gives you that nothing on the page does today: you can see the shape of your season (a run of grey 50s then a gold spike where the wildcard lands), and you can see the cost of your own decisions — a −4 sitting above a week that went well or badly.

Layout 1 · my recommendation

Timeline across the top, squad and planning underneath

The line is the header. The board sits under it, and the right-hand column is the planning column — market, chips, the moves you are considering. Insight is not a tab: the week you are standing on explains itself under the board, one line of it, and expands if you want the rest.

Layout 2 · the out-there one

The season is a document you scroll — the board follows you

Turn it ninety degrees. The weeks run down the page as a rail of cards, each one carrying its own points, rating, moves and warnings. The board is sticky beside them, and it morphs as you scroll: scroll to GW9 and the board shows the wildcard squad. Your season becomes a document you read top to bottom, with the team always in view.

What makes this different: the season stops being something you step through one week at a time and becomes something you read. The rail is also the natural phone layout — scrolling down IS moving through time, so the desktop and the phone are the same idea rather than two layouts. The risk is scroll-jacking: a board that changes as you scroll can feel like the page is arguing with you, so it would need the board to change on a deliberate stop, not continuously.

Layout 3 · for the planners

The plan as a grid: who is in your squad, and when

Fifteen rows, the next ten weeks across. Each cell is that player's fixture, coloured by difficulty. A transfer is a row ending and another starting — so a plan four weeks deep is one picture, and the thing you are actually deciding (is this run worth a hit?) is right there in the pattern.

Layout 4 · the properly mad one

Two boards: this week, and the week you are planning for

Put GW6 and GW9 side by side. You edit the right-hand one. The difference between them is your transfer plan, drawn as arrows down the middle, and the number under each board is what that squad is worth. Planning stops being "remember what you changed" and becomes "look at the two teams".

The honest case for it: the question people actually ask is "is this worth it?", and that is a comparison of two squads — which is exactly what Compare already does for two plans, pointed at two weeks of one plan instead. The honest case against: two boards is 1,360px of pitch, so on anything under a big monitor one of them is a list, and the middle column is the interesting part on every screen size. I would build this as a mode you can enter from the timeline — drag GW6 onto GW9 — rather than as the page.

Where the insight goes

Three answers, and I only believe one

a · a tab, as now

Eight panels in a column you open on purpose. Honest, and nobody opens it — it is a room you have to decide to walk into, and the decision it helps with was made two clicks ago.

b · attached to the week ✓

One line under the board about the week you are standing on, expanding to the full set. Every panel we have is already a sentence about a week — Horizon, Captain, Clashes, Watch, Template. They were only ever grouped because they had nowhere to be.

c · painted on the board

The insight becomes a lens on the pitch: shade the cards by rotation risk, ownership, form. Lovely, and it can only ever say things that are true of individuals — "GW6 is your hardest week" has nowhere to live.

Which is why b, with c as a bonus once the metric toggle grows a couple more options. The Insights tab does not disappear so much as get distributed: each panel moves next to the week or the player it is about.

What I'd build

1 as the page, 3 as what it opens into, 2 as the phone

Layout 1 is the page. The timeline across the top from GW1 to the end of your horizon, past and future on one axis, transfers and chips drawn on it. The board under it, the planning column beside it, and the week's insight as one line under the board that expands.

Pull the timeline down and it becomes layout 3 — the same weeks, the same axis, but every player's run in it and your transfers as rows starting and ending. One gesture between "the shape of my season" and "the detail of my plan".

On a phone the timeline turns ninety degrees and becomes layout 2's rail, because on a phone scrolling down is already how you move through time. Same information, same order, no second design.

And layout 4 is the thing the timeline can do that nothing else can: pick two weeks and put them side by side. Not the page — a thing you ask the page for.
